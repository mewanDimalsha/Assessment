  Key numbers:
  - 177,200 injury crashes, Jan 2012 – Aug 2025; 39.1% fatal or serious

  Chi-square test (speed zone x fatal/serious yes-no):
    chi2(8) = 2,437.3, p < 0.001, Cramér's V = 0.117
    In plain English: if speed zone and severity were unrelated, a difference this large would almost never happen by chance (p < 0.001), so they are associated.
    Cramér's V = 0.12 (0 = no link, 1 = perfect link; ~0.1 = weak): the link is real but weak - speed zone on its own explains only a small part of which crashes are severe.
    With 177,200 crashes even small differences give tiny p-values, so judge importance by Cramér's V and the odds ratios, not p.
    (Investigation 1 tested speed zone x 3 severity levels: V = 0.104.)

      - 40 km/h: 14% lower odds of a fatal/serious outcome than 60 km/h (OR 0.86, 95% CI 0.82–0.89, p < 0.001)

=== 7. Deep investigation: does the speed-zone effect hold after adjustment? ===
  Base selection (shared with investigation 6):
  Starting rows: 200,754
  Step 1: removed 3,439 crashes from the incomplete period
  Step 2: removed 4 non-injury crashes
  Step 3: removed 14,682 crashes with unknown speed zone
  Step 4: removed 5,399 crashes with unknown light condition (5,192) or road geometry (213)
  Step 5: Speed zone: removed rare categories '75 km/h' (19)
  Step 5: Road geometry: removed rare categories 'Private property' (6), 'Road closure' (5)
  Rows used: 177,200 (88.3% of all rows); 39.1% fatal or serious
  Step 6-7: tidied crash type labels; added speed bands ['≤50', '60', '70–80', '≥90']
  Step 8 (M3 only): removed 7,222 crashes with unknown or rare road class, urban/rural area or divided status (road class unknown: 6,409)

  Key numbers:
  - 177,200 crashes (M0–M2), 169,978 for M3; 39.1% fatal or serious
  - Speed-zone odds ratios vs 60 km/h, unadjusted (M0) -> fully adjusted (M3):
       40 km/h: 0.86 [0.82–0.89] -> 0.79 [0.76–0.83]
       70 km/h: 1.17 [1.12–1.22] -> 1.23 [1.18–1.28]
       80 km/h: 1.30 [1.26–1.34] -> 1.33 [1.28–1.37]
      100 km/h: 1.84 [1.79–1.89] -> 1.59 [1.52–1.66]
      110 km/h: 1.67 [1.53–1.83] -> 1.64 [1.47–1.83]
  - 100 km/h across models: M0 1.84 -> M1 1.74 -> M2 1.67 -> M3 1.59
    The extra odds at 100 km/h shrink from +84% to +59% (30% of the raw effect explained by the added factors)
  - Speed as a number (M3): each +10 km/h in the posted limit multiplies the odds by 1.126 [95% CI 1.116–1.136]
  - Model fit (cross-validated AUC): M0 0.561, M1 0.579, M2 0.609, M3 0.612

  - Within-group comparison (100 vs 60 km/h): odds ratio > 1 in 16 of 16 groups; the 95% CI is entirely above 1 in 15
      pooled within crash type        OR 1.71 [1.66–1.77]
      pooled within light condition   OR 1.84 [1.79–1.90]
      pooled within road class        OR 2.02 [1.96–2.09]
      pooled within metro vs country  OR 1.77 [1.71–1.82]
      weakest group: Dark, no street lights (OR 1.23 [1.08–1.39]); strongest: Arterial Highway (OR 2.24)
      skipped (too few 100 km/h crashes): Dark, lights off
  - Crash mix, zones <90 vs >=90 km/h: struck pedestrian 9.4% vs 0.7%; fixed object 11.9% vs 34.0%

  Findings:
  1. The speed-zone effect holds. After adding light, geometry, crash type, road class, urban/rural area and divided roads, 100 km/h zones still have 59% higher odds of a fatal/serious outcome than 60 km/h (OR 1.59), and the pattern rises steadily with the limit (1.13x odds per 10 km/h).
  2. Part of the raw effect (30%) is explained by the other factors. Crash type matters most: high-speed zones have many more fixed-object and rollover crashes and far fewer pedestrian crashes. Adjusting for it lowers the 40-50 km/h odds ratios (pedestrian crashes were masking their advantage).
  3. The effect appears inside almost every group tested (15 of 16 within-group CIs are entirely above 1), in metro and country areas and on every road class, so no single confounder explains it.
  - Road design: freeway 88% / divided 88% of 110 km/h crashes vs freeway 27% / divided 29% of 100 km/h crashes
  4. 110 km/h zones: before adjusting for road type (M1) their odds ratio (1.51) was below 100 km/h (1.74); after adding road class and divided roads (M3) it is 1.64 vs 1.59 (CIs overlap - no clear difference). 110 km/h roads are mostly divided freeways, so their lower raw severity reflects road design (barriers, no oncoming traffic) rather than the higher limit being safer.

  Possible confounders and limits:
  - Rural roads: high-speed zones are mostly rural. M3 adjusts for road class and urban/rural area, but other rural factors remain (distance to hospital and ambulance response times, older vehicles, unsealed shoulders, trees close to the road).
  - No traffic volume (exposure) data: we can compare how *severe* crashes are, not how *likely* a crash is per km travelled. Nothing here says 100 km/h roads have more crashes per trip.
  - Only injury crashes are recorded: if minor crashes on high-speed rural roads are less often reported than in the city, the FSI share there is overstated.
  - Posted limit is not actual travel speed: congestion and speeding are not measured.
  - Crash type is partly *caused* by speed (faster vehicles are more likely to leave the road and hit a fixed object), so adjusting for it removes some of the real speed effect. M2/M3 are therefore conservative estimates.
  - Not in the data: driver age and experience, alcohol/drugs, fatigue, seatbelt/helmet use, vehicle type and safety rating.

  Confidence level:
  - HIGH that higher speed zones are associated with more severe crashes: the effect is large, tightly estimated, rises steadily with the limit, and survives every adjustment and every group comparison.
  - MODERATE on the size: adjusted OR about 1.6 for 100 vs 60 km/h, but it ranges from 1.59 to 1.84 depending on the model, and adjusting for crash type may remove part of the true effect.
  - LOW that speed limits alone *cause* the difference: this is observational data with unmeasured confounders (exposure, actual speed, emergency response).
